The cards aren't thin. Most are empty.

A look at what pdf.net and iLovePDF put on a tool card, what we put on ours, and the one number that reframes the problem: 67 of our 114 tools have no description at all.

The findingIt isn't a copy-length problem

The instinct on seeing pdf.net's grid is that our blurbs are too short. That's the second problem. The first is that most cards don't have one.

Look at the four that do have copy. Word into a PDF. Slides into a PDF. Sheets into a PDF. Web page to PDF. Each one restates the tool's own name with different grammar. A person reading that learns nothing they didn't get from the title — so the line costs a row of vertical space and returns nothing.

What they actually doThe structure is the idea, not the length

pdf.net's blurbs are two clauses, and the second one is doing all the work:

Transform DOC/DOCX to PDF. Preserve formatting.
Merge JPEG photos into a single PDF file without losing quality.
Combine multiple PNG images into one PDF. Resolution maintained.
Turn iPhone HEIC photos into a shareable PDF. Opens on any device.
Clause one: what it does. Clause two: the specific thing you were afraid would go wrong.

Nobody converting a Word file is unsure what "Word to PDF" means. They're wondering whether their table will survive. Preserve formatting is the whole sale, in two words. iLovePDF does the same thing at greater length — "The converted WORD document is almost 100% accurate" — and even hedges honestly with almost.

Two additions worth making at the same time

A format-pair chip on converters. Proposed, built, then dropped. The argument was that pdf.net's illustrated icons beat flat glyphs because they show the transformation — you read DOCX→PDF before you read a word — and that a monospace chip buys most of that for no art budget. Building it showed the reasoning does not survive our own tool names. Their titles read "Convert Word to PDF", so a picture genuinely scans faster than the sentence. Ours read "Word to PDF" — the formats are already in the title, and a text chip underneath saying DOCX → PDF is precisely the restatement this page objects to. The illustration works because it is visual, not because it is redundant; a text chip keeps the redundancy and loses the visual. Dropped rather than shipped.

The lesson generalises: their pattern was worth copying, their implementation of it was not, because our titles carry information theirs do not. Custom illustrations remain the only real version of this idea, and that is an art commission, not an afternoon.

Say "on your device" in words, not a glyph. Our single biggest differentiator is currently a small crossed-out cloud in a card corner. Nobody decodes that. It's also the one thing on the card that no competitor can copy.

Why not just copy their length

They have 35 tools; we have 114. Their three-line description costs them one screen. At our count the same treatment adds roughly 340 lines of text to the grid and turns a scannable directory into a wall. The formula above stays at two lines, which holds the current row height — the cards get more useful without the page getting longer.

Your second questionDoes "Jump back in" earn its place?

Keep it, but stop looking at it. It isn't the feature you're reaching for.

It costs nothing and it's well built — jump-back-in.tsx returns null when there's no history, so a first-time visitor from search never sees it. There's no case for removing it.

But it isn't pulling its weight either, for three honest reasons:

  • It duplicates the ⌘K palette's Recent section. Two surfaces, one list.
  • Recency is a weak signal. The row in your screenshot reads Receipt scanner, Budget tracker, Webpage to PDF, Compress PDF — four unrelated errands, not a workflow. It tells you what you touched, which you already know.
  • It sits above All tools, the most valuable strip on the page, and only ever serves returning homepage visitors — a small slice when most traffic lands directly on a tool page from search.

The version worth building is already item 9 on the roadmap: a “what comes next” dock. Sequence beats recency, because it works on the first visit too. Someone who just compressed a PDF is about to send it — that's the moment for Share-safe check. Someone who merged is about to add page numbers. That row helps a stranger; a recents row can only ever help you.

ShippedWhat actually went in

  • 114 blurbs written to the formula, moved out of all-tools-directory.tsx and onto the tool itself in catalog.tsx. That file move is the actual fix: the blurbs lived in a file about how the grid looks, so nobody adding a tool row was ever asked the question.
  • A guard test, tool-blurbs.test.ts — fails the build on a missing blurb, one under five words, one over 64 characters, a duplicate, or one that says nothing the title did not. Two of its rules had to be loosened against real copy: counting echoed name words wrongly failed "Reorder before you merge", and a capital-letter rule wrongly failed iPhone and camelCase.
  • The format-pair chip was dropped, for the reason above.
  • The corner badge glyph became words — Runs in your browser / Processed on our servers — keeping all five honest states. A 15px crossed-out cloud asks the reader to already know what it means, and says nothing at all on a phone, where there is no hover to reveal the tooltip.

The blurbs are also on-page text on 114 routes, which the missing ones were contributing nothing to.
