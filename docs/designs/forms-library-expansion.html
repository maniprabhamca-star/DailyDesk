How far the forms library goes

Two questions, asked the same afternoon: “every US state has unique forms — do we have all of them? what about grouping all the states and the top countries?” and “should the forms site be new, or can we do it here in DiemDesk?”

New site, or here?

Here — as a subdirectory. Not a new domain, not a subdomain.

Our product is not a directory of forms. It is the one-click handoff into the fill engine, and that handoff cannot cross a domain.

All fifty states?

Not as a directory. As a matrix: one form, fifty states, one page.

A per-state directory is 1,500–4,500 forms and none of them verifiable. The matrix is a tenth the size and answers a sharper question.

The US library closed this morning and a job now re-reads all thirty forms from the IRS, USCIS, the Department of Labor, the VA and Social Security every Monday. So the question of what comes next is live, and it arrives with one gap already named: Georgia’s ST-5 is not there, and neither is the multijurisdiction certificate that covers most of the states that would otherwise need their own.

The claim everything protects

The revision is the one the agency publishes today, we counted the fields ourselves, and what you type never leaves your device.

That sentence — not the number of forms — is the product. pdfFiller, US Legal Forms and FormSwift all run libraries many times our size; they host copies that go stale and put a sign-up wall in front of the download. pdf.net badges its W-9 page “Official IRS form” and links irs.gov zero times. None of them can print our sentence, and that is the whole opening.

02

Why it stays inside DiemDesk

The case for a separate property is real. It loses on three counts, and the first is structural rather than commercial.

1. The handoff cannot cross a domain

A DiemDesk form page is not a directory entry with a download link. It fetches the blank form live from the agency and drops it straight into our fill tool, in the reader’s browser, with nothing uploaded. A separate site would have to either rebuild the editor — the expensive half of this company — or bounce the reader to diemdesk.com. A redirect to an unfamiliar domain, on the page where someone is about to type a Social Security number, is the worst place we could possibly spend trust.

2. A new domain starts at zero against entrenched incumbents

diemdesk.com has 176 indexed pages, an llms.txt, Markdown twins and a real sitemap. A new domain has none of it, and form queries are contested by the agencies themselves plus aggregators holding a decade of backlinks. Realistically that is twelve to eighteen months before it ranks for anything — against a subdirectory that inherits everything on day one.

3. A second surface costs what we already decided it costs

Second deploy, second CI, second nginx, second robots and sitemap and analytics, second set of legal pages, second QA matrix. This is the same arithmetic that killed the native app: the build estimate was never the problem, the permanent multiplier on every future change was.

And a subdomain is the worst of the three

Search engines treat it as semi-separate, so authority transfers less well than a subdirectory — while the operational cost is identical to a separate site. It buys the downside of both.

Worth doing now either way

One /forms hub that owns the whole family — US federal, state matrices, India, passport photos. Today the libraries are siblings with no parent, so there is nothing to point a backlink at that means “DiemDesk does forms.”

03

Directory, or matrix

The reframe that makes state coverage possible at all. Same ambition, a tenth of the surface.

Nobody needs “all Georgia forms.” They need one form and don’t know which state publishes which version of it. So the valuable unit is not a per-state folder — it is a form type read across every state at once, on a single page.

Per-state directory

Fifty folders × roughly three agencies × 10–30 forms each.

1,500–4,500 forms. Every one needs a stable URL, a version-marker concept, a field count and a page count. Unverifiable at that volume — which means we would publish the claim without being able to keep it.

Form-type × state matrix

One form type, read across every state, on one page.

~46 jurisdictions on one page, from roughly 15 PDFs to begin with. A strong hub plus thin spokes, instead of two thousand orphans competing with the agencies’ own sites.

The exemption-certificate matrix is the clearest case, and it is also where the owner’s missing form sits. The Multistate Tax Commission’s Uniform Sales & Use Tax Resale Certificate – Multijurisdiction (last revised 14 October 2022) is accepted by 36 states. Those states are one row. The handful that insist on their own certificate are the remaining cells — and ST-5 is one of them. One page then answers a question a real business asks every week: which certificate does my customer in this state actually accept?

The trap, stated plainly

Adding forms faster than we can verify them trades the moat for page count. Do that at scale and we become a smaller pdfFiller with the same weakness. Breadth has to be a function of verifiability, not of ambition — which is the honest reason the answer to “all fifty states” is no.

04

The implementation plan

Four phases, in dependency order. Each is shippable on its own and the next one is optional. The numbering carries real sequence — Phase 0 removes defects the later phases would otherwise multiply.

PHASE 0

Close the gaps in what we already publish

0 new forms~7 hdo first

Fix-before-scale — the same call already made on the passport specs. None of this adds a page; all of it stops a later phase from multiplying a defect.

  • verify:india-forms. The 32 Indian pages assert an authority and an official URL, and nothing checks that those still resolve. A link-reachability verifier on the same contract as verify:us-forms, wired into the same weekly job. They deliberately carry no field or page counts — most Indian filing is online and there is often no fillable PDF to measure, which is correct and should not be “fixed”.
  • The /forms hub. A parent for the family, and the page we build links to.
  • Print the staleness, not just the date. Each page shows when we checked. Change it to how long ago, and let it go red past a threshold. A claim that rots silently is worse than no claim at all.

PHASE 2

More countries — chosen by publishing behaviour, not by GDP

~50 forms3 countries

Two hard requirements, and a country that fails either one is simply out:

  • Forms published centrally by one authority at stable URLs.
  • The published PDF is actually fillable. If it is a scan we would be hosting a picture, and the fill tool has nothing to fill.

On that test the realistic next pair is the UK (HMRC) and Canada (CRA) — a single national authority, stable gov.uk and canada.ca URLs, English, and a clear version marker on each form. Australia (ATO) third.

Explicitly not: anything behind a login or a bot check. The State Department’s DS-11 and DS-82 are already excluded for exactly this reason and they stay excluded — we do not bypass a Cloudflare challenge, on any site, for any purpose.

PHASE 3

The form finder — the part that is actually ours

the payoffPro line

You say what you are trying to do — “hire someone in Texas”, “sell to a reseller in Georgia”, “change my address with the IRS” — and get the set of forms, in order, each one click from being filled on your own device.

No competitor can build this, because it needs the verified index and the fill engine and on-device chaining, and none of them holds all three. It is also the natural paid shape: the directory stays free and is the funnel, while the packet is Pro — multi-form, your details entered once and carried across every form in the set, chained through Saved Workflows, which is already built.

This is the phase that justifies the content work in 1 and 2. Worth keeping in view from the first data model so nothing forecloses it.

06

Keeping it in sync

The weekly job is built and proven. These are the four changes it needs before the number of forms has a comma in it.

.github/workflows/forms-sync.yml runs Mondays at 06:00 UTC, and on any push that touches the form data or the verifier — an edit is when a wrong revision is cheapest to catch. Drift fails the run red; a form that merely could not be read is emailed and left green, because one week of that is noise and the same form twice is a claim nobody has checked. First real run: forms=30 drifts=0 unchecked=0, in forty seconds.

Daily is the wrong ask. These forms change a handful of times a year, and the window that matters is “did we notice within days”, not “within hours”. Tiered by how often each issuer actually revises — derived from observed history rather than guessed:

TierExampleCadence
A — annual, predictable1040 series, state withholding certificatesWeekly Dec–Feb, monthly otherwise
B — irregularUSCIS editions, state exemption certificatesWeekly
C — stable for yearsMTC multijurisdiction certificateMonthly

The job makes about ninety requests today. At two thousand forms it would be six thousand, across a hundred and fifty hosts, many slow and some hostile. Four changes make that feasible:

  • Conditional requests. Store each form’s ETag and Last-Modified; a 304 skips the download and the parse. Six thousand heavy requests become six thousand cheap ones with perhaps twenty downloads.
  • Content hash as the primary drift signal. Hash the PDF bytes. Cheaper and more reliable than parsing a revision marker, and it catches changes the marker misses. Parse the marker only once the hash has moved.
  • Per-issuer marker adapters, not one regex. Five federal agencies already use five different version concepts — IRS Rev., IRS annual form year, USCIS edition date, DOL Revised, VA and SSA form date. Fifty states will be closer to fifty. Each issuer gets a small pluggable reader with its own test fixture, so a new state is a data row plus one adapter rather than a change to shared parsing.
  • Shard by issuer across a job matrix, so one slow state cannot time out the run and we stay polite to every host.
07

Compliance — the invariants, not the intentions

Written down because they are the kind of thing that survives the first batch and quietly lapses on the fourth.

  • We host nothing. Ever. Every file is fetched live from the issuing agency at click time. This single invariant is simultaneously the privacy story, the staleness answer and the copyright answer — the day we mirror a PDF, all three weaken at once.
  • Copyright. US federal government works are not subject to copyright (17 U.S.C. §105). State and foreign forms get no such blanket exemption — a second, independent reason live fetch is the only defensible model as we move into states and other countries.
  • Non-affiliation. Every page naming an agency carries the shared notice: not affiliated, why the name is on the page at all, and what we actually are. A test lists every file that must carry it. Non-negotiable for every new page.
  • No legal or tax advice. Pages say what a form is and who publishes it. They never say whether you should file it, or what to put on it.
  • Not a preparer. We supply a tool, not a service. Nothing may imply we review, check or submit anything on anyone’s behalf.
